Privacy

Your work stays yours.

Effective 19 November 2026. This covers Ravenry for Mac, the Android app and the iPhone app.

The short version

  • We don't collect anything. Ravenry has no accounts, and no analytics, crash reports or tracking of any kind.
  • Your sessions stay on your Mac. Your phone talks straight to your Mac, over your Wi-Fi or your own Tailscale. Your sessions never go through us.
  • One relay, for iPhone and iPad alerts. It is our only server. It passes each alert to Apple, sealed on your Mac so the relay cannot read it, and keeps nothing.
  • A few things leave your devices, only when you use them: the alerts you set up, the update check, and the Claude Code or Codex you already use. Each one is below.

What stays on your devices

On your Mac

  • Ravenry reads your Claude Code and Codex sessions (their transcripts and what their terminals show) to put them on the board. It reads them on your Mac and copies them nowhere.
  • It keeps your projects, briefs, decisions, ideas, pages, plans, settings, the phones you paired and its logs in ~/.ravenry and ~/Library/Application Support/Ravenry. Bot tokens, webhooks and alert topics are in a file only your user can read.
  • Photos and voice notes you send from your phone land in that project's folder on your Mac, in .inbox, which git ignores. The phone deletes its copy once it is sent. Files you attach from your phone go there too, and nowhere else. Your phone's own file picker hands Ravenry only the files you pick, so attaching needs no permission.
  • A secret you type goes straight to the command that asked for it. It is never written down: not to a file, a log or an alert.
  • If you turn on rule suggestions, Ravenry keeps the messages you typed in your sessions in ~/.ravenry/repeats, since Claude Code deletes its own transcripts after 30 days.
  • When you add another machine over ssh, the board reaches it over your own ssh connection.

On your phone

  • The app keeps which Mac it is paired with and a sign-in token, encrypted with a key that never leaves the phone. It is never backed up or moved to a new phone: a new phone pairs again.
  • The iPhone and iPad app makes a key for its alerts when you turn them on. The private half never leaves the device; your Mac gets the public half, to seal each alert so only that device can open it.
  • When it pairs, the app gives your Mac an install id, so pairing the same phone again replaces its old entry instead of adding one. On iPhone and iPad it is a random number kept on the device; on Android it is made from the id Android gives this app on this phone. A phone's browser gets a random one in a cookie. Your Mac keeps only a hash of it, and it goes nowhere else.

What leaves them, and to whom

Your phone and your Mac
They talk directly, encrypted, and the phone only trusts your Mac's own key. On your Wi-Fi, or through Tailscale when you turn on Away from this Mac's Wi-Fi.
Tailscale
Only with Away from this Mac's Wi-Fi on. Ravenry joins your own Tailscale network as a device called ravenry, so Tailscale sees that device, your account and its addresses, as it does for any of your devices. What your phone and Mac say to each other is encrypted end to end. Ravenry turns off Tailscale's log uploads. Tailscale's own privacy policy applies.
Android alerts
Through UnifiedPush and the app you pick for it, such as ntfy. Each alert is encrypted on your Mac for your phone alone, so the service that carries it sees only scrambled bytes, their size and the time. With no such app, alerts come over the connection the Ravenry app keeps to your Mac.
iPhone and iPad alerts, through the Ravenry app

Apple only lets an app's own server wake it, so these go through the Ravenry relay, which we run on Cloudflare, and then Apple's push service. Your Mac seals each alert for your device before it leaves, so the relay and Apple see only scrambled bytes. For each alert the relay sees:

  • your device's push token, an address Apple gave the app on that device (not your name, number or Apple account);
  • the sealed bytes, their size and the time;
  • the network address it came from, as any server does.

It keeps none of it: it has no storage and writes no logs. To stop abuse it counts requests per device and per address for one minute, by a one-way hash of each, in Cloudflare's memory. Apple sees the push token, the sealed bytes and a fixed line, "Open Ravenry to see this alert.", which is what your device shows if it ever cannot open one. Cloudflare's and Apple's own privacy policies apply. With the app's alerts off, nothing goes to the relay.

iPhone alerts, through ntfy

Sent to ntfy.sh unless you set your own ntfy server in Alerts, and readable there in plain text. So an alert says little:

  • a title with the project's name and what it waits for, like "paper-boat asks". For some, a tab's name;
  • a fixed line, like "Answer on the board." or "On (your Mac's name). Run or refuse on the board.";
  • a link to your Mac's board on your Wi-Fi.

Never the question, the command, the agent's words or a secret. Each iPhone gets its own topic, a long random name that works like a password: anyone who knows it can read those alerts. Remove the iPhone in Alerts to retire its topic.

Telegram, Discord, Slack or ntfy
Only if you set one up in Alerts. The same short alert goes to that service, with the bot token or webhook you gave: the project's name, what it waits for, which Mac, and the board's link. Never the session's text. That service's own policy applies.
GitHub, for updates
Ravenry for Mac checks GitHub, where its releases live, a little after it starts and every 4 hours, and downloads a new version from there. The check says which version you have and nothing about you. GitHub sees your IP address, as with any download. The Android app does not check: you download a new version yourself.
Claude Code and Codex

Ravenry runs the Claude Code and Codex you installed, signed in to your own account. What they send to Anthropic or OpenAI is between you and them.

Ravenry also asks them a few things itself, on your login: when you use the Ideas thread, mockups or Plans, and for rule suggestions, which are off unless you turn them on. Then, once a day, it sends the messages you typed in your sessions, to find the corrections you keep repeating. Ravenry has no key of its own and talks to Anthropic or OpenAI no other way.

Permissions, and why

Camera
To scan the pairing code your Mac shows. On Android, also to take a photo for an agent, and on iPhone when you pick Take Photo under Attach. The photo goes to your Mac and nowhere else.
Microphone
On Android, to record a voice note for an agent, which goes to your Mac and nowhere else. Voice typing in an answer uses your phone's own voice typing, under its own terms.
Face ID or fingerprint
To check it's you before a command runs, or before an agent types into another tab, from your phone. Your phone does the check; Ravenry only learns yes or no.
Notifications
To show you alerts.
Local network
On iPhone, to reach your Mac on your Wi-Fi.
On your Mac
Ravenry asks iTerm2 and cmux about their tabs, to show your sessions. It is also a terminal, so macOS may ask for the camera, microphone or contacts on behalf of a program you run in it. Ravenry itself never uses them.

Keeping it, and deleting it

  • We hold nothing about you, so there is nothing for us to delete.
  • On your Mac, choose Uninstall Ravenry… in the Ravenry menu and tick "Also delete Ravenry's own data". Or delete ~/.ravenry and ~/Library/Application Support/Ravenry yourself. Photos, voice notes and files you sent stay in each project's .inbox folder until you delete them.
  • On your phone, Sign out in the app's settings, or uninstall the app: what it kept goes with it.
  • ntfy, Telegram, Discord and Slack keep the alerts they carried by their own rules. The Ravenry relay keeps nothing; Apple keeps an alert for a device that is off for up to an hour, then drops it.

Children

Ravenry is a tool for developers and isn't made for children. It collects nothing from anyone, children included.

Changes

If what Ravenry does with your data changes, this page changes first, with a new date at the top.
