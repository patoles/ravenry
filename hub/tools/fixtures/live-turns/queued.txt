  Clouds form when water vapor in rising air cools until it condenses into tiny droplets or ice
  crystals around particles like dust or salt. Their shapes depend on how the air moves: cumulus
  clouds are puffy and piled up from convection on warm days, while stratus clouds are flat, grey
  sheets that spread across the sky. Higher up, cirrus clouds are thin, wispy ice crystals that
  often signal a change in weather.

  Clouds do more than decorate the sky. They reflect sunlight back to space and trap heat radiating
  from the ground, so they shape both daytime temperatures and nighttime warmth. They also move
  water around the planet, since precipitation falls from them and returns to rivers, lakes, and
  oceans. Because of this, cloud cover is one of the hardest things for climate models to represent
  accurately.
